Read this first

+

Four things decide whether you own a business or a job. Miss one and you're stuck in the truck.

Someone else can do the work

If you're the best technician, you can't leave. Hire and train delivery people from month one, and write the checklist for every recurring task. Undocumented work can't be delegated.

The phone rings without you

Recurring contracts and route-based demand beat relationship selling. If customers buy because they know you, a manager can't replace you.

The license isn't attached to your name

Many trades need a licensed "qualifying party." If that's you personally, you're chained to it. Pick work where the license rides with the technician, or hire a qualifier as a W-2 employee.

The margin can carry a manager

This is the one that kills people. A general manager costs $70K–$95K all-in. At 8% net margin you need a million dollars in sales just to afford them.

What revenue you need before you can leave

Business typeNet marginRevenue to afford a GM
Recurring residential services, storage, compliance30–40%$350K–$600K
Commercial janitorial, most trades10–20%$700K–$1.2M

Pay a GM a real base ($60K–$80K) plus 5–8% of net profit. Tie the bonus to margin, not just volume, or they'll buy revenue with your money. Add a 5% cut of the eventual sale price and they'll stay.

What "day one" actually means

+

Without a loan or an acquisition, there's exactly one way to have a business that's staffed from the start.

The asset is the employee

ATMs, vending machines, micro-markets, storage lots, trailers, billboards. Nobody clocks in — you service them on a schedule. Eight of the ninety ideas here work this way. Income per unit is modest, so you win on unit count, but it's genuinely hands-off from week one.

Everything else takes 12–36 months, and that's the honest answer

Year one you do the work. Year two you hire and train the lead. Year three you review numbers on Fridays. Buying a staffed business would have skipped straight to year three — building it yourself means you are the first employee. Budget for that.

Pick the shortest ramp you can live with

Commercial janitorial, carpet cleaning, and pet waste hit 12–15 months because the work is simple to teach and the schedule is predictable. Mobile grooming and mobile welding take 28–30 because you have to hire a skill, not a body. The timeline bar on every card below is the real filter.

What a customer costs

+

Cost per click is a vanity number. These are the two that decide whether a business works.

The formula

True cost per customer = lead cost ÷ your low-end close rate. Then: jobs to payback = that number ÷ (ticket × gross margin). Run every idea through this before you spend anything.

Same $53 lead, three very different businesses

ChannelCost/leadLow closeTrue cost/customer
Google Local Services$5315%$353
Non-branded Google Ads$14915%$993
Angi / Thumbtack (shared)$505%$1,000

At a $1,800 ticket and 25% margin you clear $450 a job — the LSA customer pays for himself immediately, the shared-lead customer never does. Cost per booked job in the real world: LSA $168, Thumbtack $250, Angi $542. Shared leads go to four or five contractors at once, which is why they close at 5–10% instead of 20–25%.

Answer in five minutes or don't bother

The Oldroyd lead-response study found that answering within 5 minutes makes you 21× more likely to qualify a lead than waiting 30. Separate industry surveys put the share of customers who hire whoever responds first at around 78%. Both point the same direction: speed is cheaper than ad spend, and most of your competition is bad at it.

Recurring lets you pay 10× more

A junk removal customer bills $400 once, so your cost to get him has to stay under about $200. A pest control customer bills $150 up front but $500 a year for three-plus years — a $233 acquisition cost pays back inside year one and returns 6:1 after that. Same trade, same city, completely different math.

Hangers beat EDDM because you skip apartments and rentals — one lawn operator found nearly a third of his EDDM spend hit non-owner households. Three things move the number: tight clustering (route density cuts your fulfillment cost), one offer with a deadline and a guarantee, and re-dropping the same streets 3–4 times a season. A single pass underperforms badly.

You’re paying in time instead of money

Buying a book of accounts would skip all of this. Since you’re building, every customer gets earned one at a time — so pick a category where the first ten come cheap. A $47 cleaning lead and a $149 non-branded HVAC lead are not the same starting line.

Is it already too crowded?

+

The raw number of competitors is the wrong thing to fear. What matters is whether they're one person with a truck or a company with forty employees.

Why 692,777 lawn companies isn't the problem you think

Almost every one of them is a solo operator who can't answer the phone while he's mowing. You're not competing with 692,777 businesses — you're competing with 692,777 individuals, and none of them has a dispatcher. Meanwhile a category with only 15,000 garage door companies is far harder to enter, because private equity is buying them at 16× earnings and outspending you on every lead.

Open “Can I get in?” on any card

Each one gives you a straight answer, a demand meter running Soft to Surging, and a search to run in your own town. That last part matters most — national numbers can't see your county. Ten minutes on Google Maps tells you more about your market than any statistic in this guide.

Where the window is closing

Private equity has already taken fire and life safety (129 acquisitions in 2023; one buyer closed 57 deals in 2025 alone) and is moving fast on garage doors (26 deals in 2025, one platform sold for over $800M). Self-storage belongs to the REITs. Vending is shrinking outright — operator count down 3.8% a year. These aren't markets you enter with $75K.

The signal that a market is actually busy

Nobody publishes "weeks booked out." The closest honest proxies are a shrinking competitor count against rising revenue, and a documented shortage of people to do the work. Where both show up together — septic, chimney, mobile mechanics, ag drone spraying — the existing operators are turning work away.

Two different ways to count competitors

Some counts here are Census figures for companies with actual payroll. Others are industry estimates that include every solo operator with a truck. That gap is enormous — janitorial is roughly 66,000 employer companies or well over a million businesses depending on which you use. Each card says which one it's showing, because the distinction is the whole difference between “crowded” and “crowded with people who can't answer the phone.”

National numbers, local reality

These are US-wide figures. Pool service, RV storage, ag spraying and septic swing enormously by region — a category that reads "room to enter" nationally can be packed in your county, or wide open. Check your own market before committing.

    Don't buy these

    +

    Categories that get sold as passive and reliably aren't.

    • Single-unit restaurants and QSR. Margins too thin to carry a manager on one location. Constant owner presence.
    • Single-unit boutique fitness. A $1.5M investment that can't cover management plus debt service. Only works multi-unit.
    • Solo-skill trades with no hiring pool. Mobile grooming, high-end custom work. If you can't hire the skill, you can't leave.
    • Anything where you personally hold the license and your state won't let an employee qualify it.
    • 60%-in-one-quarter seasonal. You'll be all hands at peak no matter who manages.
    • Agencies, brokerages, consulting. You are the rainmaker. There's nothing to sell.
    • "Passive" franchise pitches. Fully hands-off from day one is uncommon and carries extra risk. Read Item 19, then call ten franchisees.
    • Attended laundromats and wash-dry-fold add-ons. Verify the actual labor model — WDF eats 25–35% of revenue in wages and kills the passivity.

    Your next four moves

    +

    All cash, no debt, no acquisition. That means revenue has to fund every step.

    Weeks 1–4 · Pick one and check the lead math

    Choose from the fifteen above. Before spending anything, run the section 04 formula on your own metro: lead cost ÷ your low-end close rate. If a customer costs more than one job's gross profit and the work doesn't recur, pick something else.

    Months 1–6 · Sell before you spend

    Get signed recurring contracts or a full route before buying the second truck. Hold back $15K–$20K of the $75K as working capital — with no credit line behind you, running out of cash is the failure mode, not running out of customers.

    Months 6–18 · Hire the technician, not the manager

    Your first hire does the work so you can sell. A manager comes much later — you need $350K–$600K in revenue on a 30–40% margin before one is affordable. Trying to hire management early is how self-funded businesses stall out.

    Months 18–36 · Promote a lead and step back

    Write the SOPs as you go, not at the end. Put in field-service software, hand the crew to a working lead on base plus 5–8% of net profit, and move yourself to a weekly numbers review.
